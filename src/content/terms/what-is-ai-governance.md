AI governance is the set of rules, roles and routines an organisation uses to decide which AI it uses, what for, and who answers for it. It is not one law, one product or one department; it is how a whole company makes AI decisions on purpose. Without it, a tool someone signed up for on Monday can be writing customer emails by Friday, and nobody decided that.

## Rules, roles and routines

- **Rules** say what is allowed, for example which data may go into which tool.
- **Roles** say who decides, and who is accountable when something goes wrong.
- **Routines** are the checks that keep happening after launch, not just once.

The rest of this article follows one ordinary week at a made-up company, where the sales team starts using an AI note-taker for customer calls.

## Monday: what AI are we using?

The note-taker is useful and cheap, and nobody else knows about it. AI tools used at work without the company knowing are often called **shadow AI**.

The first governance question is simple: what AI are we actually using? The [NIST AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework), a voluntary guide from the United States, lists a way to keep an inventory of AI systems as a basic outcome. You cannot manage a tool you do not know exists, so step one is a list.

## Tuesday: who decides?

On Tuesday, someone asks whether customer calls are even allowed to go into this tool, and nobody knows who should answer.

Every AI tool needs an owner: someone who knows what it does and can say yes or no. The NIST framework asks for roles and responsibilities to be written down and clear to everyone, and says executive leadership should take responsibility for decisions about AI risks. The owner is not the person who found the tool; it is the person accountable for it.

## Wednesday: how risky is it?

On Wednesday, the owner looks at the risk. A tool that summarises internal notes is one thing; a tool that sorts job applications is something else. Governance does not treat every AI tool the same. It asks what could go wrong, for whom, and how badly.

The EU AI Act works the same way: it [sorts AI by risk](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai), not by how clever it is. CV-sorting software for recruitment is one of its examples of high-risk AI, with strict rules that apply from 2 December 2027. Chatbots have had to tell people they are talking to an AI since August 2026 ([Article 50](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/)).

## Thursday: the frameworks you will hear about

Two names come up a lot. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework was released in January 2023. It is voluntary and has four functions: govern, map, measure and manage. *Govern* is what this week has been about, and it runs through the other three ([framework core](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/5-sec-core/)).

The second is [ISO/IEC 42001](https://www.iso.org/standard/42001), an international standard published in December 2023. It sets requirements for an AI management system, using the familiar plan, do, check, act cycle. Neither is needed to start, but both make a useful checklist once you do.

## Friday: a clear yes, with limits

On Friday, the note-taker gets a decision: yes for internal calls; not for calls with customers until they have agreed to it.

That is the point people miss. Governance is not mainly about saying no. It makes a clear yes possible, with limits everyone knows. The NIST framework also covers the other end: someone needs to be responsible for switching an AI system off if it starts doing things it should not. A company that can switch AI off safely can say yes to it with more confidence.

## Where to start

Four small things are enough to begin: a list of the AI tools you use, an owner for each one, a short policy on what data may go into which tool, and a regular review with a way to switch a tool off.

## Recap

- AI governance is the rules, roles and routines that decide which AI a company uses, for what, and who answers for it.
- Start with an inventory: you cannot manage a tool you do not know exists.
- Give every tool an accountable owner.
- Match the checks to the risk; the EU AI Act sorts AI the same way.
- Governance makes a clear yes possible, with limits and a way to switch a tool off.
